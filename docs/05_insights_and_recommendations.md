# Step 8 - Business Insights and Recommendations

Every figure in this document comes from `docs/03_analysis_findings.md`, which is
generated by executing `sql/02_analysis_queries.sql` against the warehouse. The
query that produced each number is named in brackets. Nothing here is estimated.

Population: **24,918 incidents / 141,707 process events**, Feb 2016 - Feb 2017,
from an anonymised ServiceNow-style incident management event log.

---

## Read this first: three limits on what the data can support

These are stated up front because they change how several findings must be read.

**1. Only four months carry meaningful volume.** [Q02]

| Period | Incidents |
|---|---|
| Feb 2016 | 207 |
| Mar - May 2016 | 8,995 / 7,934 / 7,508 |
| Jun 2016 - Feb 2017 | between 5 and 94 per month |

99.0% of all incidents were opened in Mar-May 2016. The months after May 2016
contain too few incidents to support a trend claim - a single incident moves the
monthly attainment figure by several percentage points. **The monthly trend line
is presented in the report for completeness and is explicitly annotated as not
statistically meaningful after May 2016.** All cross-sectional findings below
(priority, state, handoffs, variants) draw on the full population and are not
affected by this.

**2. `closed_at` is unreliable as a duration endpoint.** A large share of
incidents share a common closure timestamp, which is the signature of a bulk
administrative close rather than real closure activity. Cycle time is therefore
measured `opened_at` -> `resolved_at` throughout, and the 1,556 incidents with no
`resolved_at` are excluded from duration metrics by a single explicit flag
(`has_valid_resolution_time`) rather than being silently counted as zero.

**3. The knowledge-article result is not causal.** [Q10]

| | Incidents | SLA attainment | Median resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knowledge article used | 3,551 | 26.75% | 77.6 h |
| No knowledge article | 21,367 | 69.51% | 12.2 h |

Read naively this says knowledge articles make things worse. The far more likely
explanation is selection: articles get attached to the incidents that are already
hard, long-running, and heading for a breach. This observational data cannot
separate the two, so **no recommendation is made on knowledge management**. The
honest output is a test design, not a conclusion - see Recommendation 5.

## Finding 1 - SLA attainment is 63.4%, and the priority scheme is inverted

Overall: **15,803 met / 9,115 breached = 63.42% attainment**. [Q01]

| Priority | Incidents | % of volume | SLA attainment | % of all breaches | Median resolution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 - Critical | 270 | 1.08% | **1.85%** | 2.91% | 80.2 h |
| 2 - High | 408 | 1.64% | **0.49%** | 4.45% | 38.2 h |
| 3 - Moderate | 23,466 | 94.17% | 64.54% | 91.29% | 21.6 h |
| 4 - Low | 774 | 3.11% | 84.11% | 1.35% | 5.0 h |

[Q04]

Attainment falls monotonically as priority rises. Critical incidents meet their
target 1.85% of the time; High incidents 0.49% - effectively never. Two readings
are possible and the data distinguishes them: if high-priority work were simply
being neglected it would also be slow in absolute terms, but Critical resolves in
a median 80 hours and High in 38 hours, against Moderate's 21.6 hours. The
high-priority tiers *are* worked faster per unit of urgency than the target
implies - the targets themselves are not achievable by this process.

**This is a target-setting problem, not only a performance problem.** A tier that
is missed 99.5% of the time has stopped conveying information; it cannot be used
to prioritise, because everything in it is already late.

The second half of the table matters just as much: **Moderate is 94% of volume
and 91% of all breaches.** Any intervention aimed only at Critical and High can
address at most 7% of breaches. The volume problem is in the middle tier.

## Finding 2 - Half of all elapsed time is post-resolution administration

| State | Category | Total elapsed hours | % of elapsed | Median dwell |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Resolved | Terminal | 3,726,123 | **47.43%** | 120.6 h |
| Active | Working | 1,414,354 | 18.01% | 0.55 h |
| Awaiting User Info | Waiting | 1,230,444 | 15.66% | 17.9 h |
| New | Working | 1,084,554 | 13.81% | 0.55 h |
| Awaiting Vendor | Waiting | 270,924 | 3.45% | 76.8 h |
| Awaiting Problem | Waiting | 92,949 | 1.18% | 24.1 h |
| Closed | Terminal | 23,577 | 0.30% | 43.8 h |
| Awaiting Evidence | Waiting | 12,296 | 0.16% | 81.1 h |

[Q05]

The largest single block of elapsed time - 47% - is incidents sitting in
**Resolved**, waiting to be Closed. This is the `closed_at` artifact described
above: it is administrative closure lag, not work, and it should not be read as a
service failure. It is reported rather than hidden because a bottleneck chart
that silently drops its largest bar is not an honest chart.

Excluding terminal states, the operational picture is:

- **Active: 18.0% of elapsed time**, but a median dwell of 0.55 hours against a
  mean of 36.5 hours. A 66x gap between median and mean means the typical
  incident passes through Active in half an hour while a minority stall there for
  days. Active is not uniformly slow; it has a stalling tail.
- **Awaiting User Info: 15.7%** across 14,642 occurrences, median 17.9 hours.
  This is the largest genuine queue, and it is time the resolver team does not
  control.
- **Awaiting Vendor: only 707 occurrences but a mean dwell of 383 hours.** Rare
  and extreme. Low priority by volume, but each occurrence costs roughly two
  working weeks.

Overall the split is **31.8% Working / 20.5% Waiting / 47.7% Terminal**. [Q06]
Of the time the process is actually live, roughly two hours in five are spent
blocked on somebody outside the resolver team.

**Why this distinction matters:** waiting time does not respond to adding
resolvers. Working time does. Treating them as one number would point at the
wrong remedy.

## Finding 3 - Every handoff costs measurably, and the cost compounds

| Reassignments | Incidents | % of incidents | SLA attainment | Median resolution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 13,549 | 54.37% | **78.39%** | 0.68 h |
| 1 | 6,238 | 25.03% | 57.07% | 42.1 h |
| 2 | 2,283 | 9.16% | 39.86% | 103.0 h |
| 3 | 1,234 | 4.95% | 32.90% | 146.1 h |
| 4 | 695 | 2.79% | 25.18% | 186.1 h |
| 5+ | 919 | 3.69% | **14.25%** | 292.1 h |

[Q13]

This is the cleanest gradient in the dataset. Attainment falls from 78.4% to
14.3% and median resolution rises from 0.68 hours to 292 hours - a **430x**
increase - as handoffs accumulate. The relationship is monotonic across all six
buckets, which is what distinguishes it from noise.

**45.6% of incidents are reassigned at least once.** The first handoff is the
most expensive single event in the process: it costs 21 percentage points of
attainment and takes the median from under an hour to nearly two days. That
gap is far larger than any difference between the teams themselves.

Causation runs both ways - hard incidents get reassigned, and reassignment also
delays them - so the honest framing is that **first-time routing accuracy is the
highest-leverage measurable control in this process**, not that handoffs alone
cause the delay.

## Finding 4 - Process conformance predicts outcome better than any attribute

188 distinct process variants exist across 24,918 incidents.

| | Variants | Incidents | % of volume | SLA attainment | Median resolution | Events/incident |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Top 5 variants | 5 | 20,649 | 82.87% | **70.49%** | **4.8 h** | 5.0 |
| Long-tail variants | 183 | 4,269 | 17.13% | **29.23%** | **181.4 h** | 9.1 |

[Q12]

Incidents on one of the five standard paths resolve in a median 4.8 hours and
meet SLA 70% of the time. Incidents on any of the other 183 paths take **38x
longer** and meet SLA 29% of the time. 17% of volume sits in the long tail and
generates a disproportionate share of both breaches and handling effort.

The individual variants show exactly where conformance breaks: [Q11]

| Rank | Path | Incidents | SLA | Median |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | New -> Resolved -> Closed | 6,282 | 77.83% | 1.6 h |
| 2 | New -> Active -> Resolved -> Closed | 5,493 | 58.60% | 34.6 h |
| 3 | Active -> Resolved -> Closed | 5,361 | 82.76% | 0.3 h |
| 4 | Resolved -> Closed | 1,816 | 83.70% | 0.1 h |
| 5 | New -> Active -> **Awaiting User Info** -> Resolved -> Closed | 1,697 | 28.87% | 189.2 h |
| 6 | New -> **Awaiting User Info** -> Resolved -> Closed | 1,520 | 35.20% | 148.4 h |
| 7 | Active -> **Awaiting User Info** -> Resolved -> Closed | 861 | 24.51% | 198.9 h |

The pattern is unambiguous. **Every path without an `Awaiting User Info` step
attains 58-84%. Every path with one attains 24-35%.** A single wait-for-customer
step is worth roughly 40 percentage points of SLA attainment and turns a
sub-two-day incident into a week-long one.

This connects Finding 2 and Finding 4: `Awaiting User Info` is both the largest
genuine queue by elapsed time and the single discriminating step between fast and
slow paths.

## Finding 5 - Rework is rare but catastrophic

| | Incidents | % | SLA attainment | Median resolution | Events/incident |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reopened at least once | 275 | 1.10% | 24.36% | 236.6 h | 15.1 |
| Never reopened | 24,643 | 98.90% | 63.86% | 21.2 h | 5.6 |

[Q18]

Only 1.1% of incidents are reopened, which is a genuinely good first-time-fix
rate. But a reopened incident takes **11x longer**, consumes **2.7x the process
events**, and meets SLA a quarter of the time. At this volume the aggregate cost
is modest; the finding matters as a quality signal rather than a capacity one.

## Finding 6 - Resolver capability varies more than team capability

| Cohort | Resolvers | Incidents | % of volume | SLA attainment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Top 10 resolvers | 10 | 9,865 | 39.75% | **69.40%** |
| All other resolvers | 206 | 14,954 | 60.25% | **47.01%** |

[Q15]

Ten resolvers out of 216 handle nearly 40% of all volume and attain 69% against
47% for everybody else. That is a 22-point spread on the highest-volume cohort -
the people doing the *most* work are also the ones doing it best, which rules out
the usual explanation that the top cohort is simply cherry-picking easy tickets.

At assignment-group level (groups with 100+ incidents) the worst performers are
Group 12 (14.4% attainment, 309 h median), Group 3 (16.8%, 313 h) and Group 10
(25.1%, 402 h median across 343 incidents). [Q08] Their reassignment rates
(0.86-1.14 average) are close to the overall mean, so the gap is not explained by
handoffs alone.

**Concentration risk:** 40% of throughput depends on ten individuals.

## Finding 7 - Two counterintuitive results worth stating carefully

**Out-of-hours incidents perform better, not worse.** [Q17]

| Window | Incidents | SLA attainment | Median resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opened in business hours | 20,610 | 61.55% | 24.1 h |
| Opened out of hours | 4,308 | **72.35%** | **6.1 h** |

Incidents raised outside 08:00-18:00 resolve four times faster and attain 11
points higher. The most plausible explanation is composition rather than
staffing: out-of-hours volume is likely dominated by automated or
monitoring-generated tickets with narrow, repeatable resolutions. The data
supports the observation but not the explanation, so **this is reported as a
question for the process owner, not as a finding to act on.**

**Channel differences are real but thinly evidenced.** [Q09] Phone carries 24,688
of 24,918 incidents at 63.5% attainment. Email (59 incidents, 33.9%, 146 h median)
and Self service (158 incidents, 56.3%, 50.8 h) look worse, but at those volumes
the confidence interval is wide enough that no operational decision should rest
on them. IVR and Direct opening (9 and 4 incidents) are reported for completeness
only.

## Recommendations

Ordered by expected impact against implementation cost. Each names the finding it
rests on and how it would be measured.

### 1. Re-baseline the Critical and High SLA targets - then manage to them

**From Finding 1.** Targets missed 98-99.5% of the time carry no information and
cannot drive prioritisation. Set the target from the observed achievable
distribution (e.g. the current P50 or P75 for that tier), publish it, and only
then treat a breach as an exception worth investigating.

*Measured by:* Critical/High attainment moving off the floor; breach volume in
those tiers becoming small enough to review case by case.
*Cost:* governance discussion, no system change.
*Caution:* this improves the honesty of the metric, not the underlying service.
It must be communicated as a re-baseline, never as an improvement.

### 2. Attack first-time routing accuracy on Moderate-priority incidents

**From Findings 1 and 3.** Moderate is 94% of volume and 91% of breaches, and
the first handoff costs 21 points of attainment. Routing is where those two
findings meet. Use the dominant misroutes visible in the group-level data to
tighten assignment rules at intake for the highest-volume categories.

*Measured by:* the 0-reassignment share moving above its current 54.4%.
A 5-point shift moves roughly 1,250 incidents from the 57% bucket to the 78%
bucket - on the order of **265 additional incidents meeting SLA**.
*Cost:* routing rule changes at intake; no new tooling.

### 3. Put a response clock and an escalation path on `Awaiting User Info`

**From Findings 2 and 4.** This is the largest genuine queue (15.7% of elapsed
time, 14,642 occurrences) and the single step that separates 58-84% paths from
24-35% paths. It is also currently unmanaged - median dwell 17.9 hours with a
long tail.

Concretely: an automatic chase at a defined interval, and an auto-resolve or
auto-escalate rule when the interval expires twice, so incidents cannot sit
indefinitely against a clock the resolver team does not control.

*Measured by:* median dwell in `Awaiting User Info`, and attainment on the
variants that contain it (currently 24-35%).
*Cost:* workflow change in the ITSM tool - directly in scope for a BPM team.
*Note:* if SLA clocks do not already pause during customer wait, that is the
cheaper fix and should be checked first.

### 4. Review the three lowest-performing assignment groups, and de-risk the top 10 resolvers

**From Finding 6.** Groups 12, 3 and 10 attain 14-25% with 250-400 hour medians
and near-average reassignment rates, so the cause is local rather than structural.
Separately, ten people carrying 40% of volume at a 22-point premium is a
single-point-of-failure worth documenting: capture what the top cohort does
differently before it leaves with them.

*Measured by:* the spread between best and worst group attainment; the share of
volume handled outside the top 10 cohort.
*Cost:* management attention; knowledge capture time.

### 5. Do not act on the knowledge-article finding - test it instead

**From the caveats section.** The 26.75% vs 69.51% gap is almost certainly
selection bias. Withdrawing knowledge articles on the strength of it would be a
serious error. The correct next step is a controlled comparison - match incidents
on category, priority and reassignment count, then compare - or a prospective
trial on a single category.

*Cost:* one analysis, no operational change.
*Why it is listed:* recognising which findings are not actionable is part of the
analysis, not a gap in it.

### 6. Close the Resolved -> Closed gap in the source system

**From Finding 2.** 47% of elapsed time is post-resolution administration, and
the bulk-close pattern means closure timestamps carry no analytical information.
Fixing the closure process would both shorten the reported lifecycle and restore
`closed_at` as a usable measurement endpoint for future analysis.

*Measured by:* median Resolved -> Closed dwell; the disappearance of shared
closure timestamps.
*Cost:* ITSM configuration.

## What this analysis cannot tell you

- **Cost.** There is no effort, rate or headcount data, so no finding here can be
  converted into a currency figure. Every quantification above is in incidents,
  hours or percentage points.
- **Causality.** This is observational process data. Findings 3, 4 and 7 in
  particular describe association. The handoff and conformance gradients are
  strong and monotonic enough to act on as management signals; they are not
  experimental evidence.
- **Anything after May 2016.** See the caveats section.
- **Why groups differ.** The data shows which groups underperform, not why.
  That requires the process owner.
- **SLA target definitions.** `made_sla` is supplied as an outcome flag; the
  underlying target durations are not in the dataset. Finding 1's conclusion that
  targets are unachievable rests on the observed breach rate, not on a comparison
  against a stated target value.
